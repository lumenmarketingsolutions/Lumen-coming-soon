12:T1d3c,<p>An email nurture sequence is a series of automated emails sent to a lead who has not booked yet. For a service business, a good sequence answers the questions that stop people from booking, shows proof that you do good work, and asks for the appointment with one clear button in every email. It works best alongside text and phone follow up, and it should stop the moment the lead books.</p>
<p>Most leads are not ready to book the day they reach out. Some are comparing quotes. Some are waiting on a spouse. Some are planning a project for next spring. Email keeps you in front of them without your team making ten calls.</p>
<h2>When email nurture makes sense</h2>
<p>Email does the most work for services where people think before buying:</p>
<ul>
<li>Roofing, remodeling, epoxy floors, landscaping projects and other jobs over a few thousand dollars.</li>
<li>Med spa treatments, especially first time injectables, laser and body contouring.</li>
<li>Lenders, insurance, law firms and real estate, where trust and timing matter.</li>
</ul>
<p>For urgent services like a burst pipe, texting and calling matter more. Email still helps for estimates that do not close on the spot.</p>
<h2>The building blocks of a nurture email</h2>
<p>Every email in the sequence should have:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>A clear subject line</strong> that sounds like a person wrote it.</li>
<li><strong>One idea per email.</strong> One question answered, one story told or one objection handled.</li>
<li><strong>Proof.</strong> A review, a before and after photo, a short project story.</li>
<li><strong>One call to action.</strong> &quot;Pick a time for your free estimate,&quot; with a link straight to your calendar.</li>
<li><strong>A real sender.</strong> &quot;Mike at [Company]&quot; gets opened more often than &quot;noreply.&quot;</li>
</ol>
<p>Keep emails short. Most people read on their phone. Three to six short paragraphs is plenty.</p>
<h2>A sample 14 day sequence for a home service business</h2>
<p>This outline assumes a lead requested an estimate but did not book. Text and calls continue alongside it.</p>
<p><strong>Day 0, right away: &quot;Got your request, [Name]&quot;</strong>
Confirm you received it, say who will call and when, and include a booking link in case they prefer to pick a time themselves. Add a photo of the owner or crew.</p>
<p><strong>Day 1: &quot;What to expect at your estimate&quot;</strong>
Walk through the visit step by step: how long it takes, what you will measure, whether there is any cost, and when they get a written quote. This removes uncertainty, which is a big reason people delay.</p>
<p><strong>Day 3: &quot;How the [Smith] family&#39;s garage floor turned out&quot;</strong>
Tell one short project story with photos. The problem, what you did, what the customer said. Link to more reviews.</p>
<p><strong>Day 5: &quot;The question we get most&quot;</strong>
Answer your most common objection. For roofing it might be insurance claims. For epoxy it might be how long the floor lasts. For HVAC it might be repair versus replace.</p>
<p><strong>Day 7: &quot;Are you comparing quotes?&quot;</strong>
Give honest advice on how to compare contractors: licensing, insurance, warranty terms, what is included. Buyers trust the company that helps them compare.</p>
<p><strong>Day 10: &quot;Financing options&quot;</strong> (if you offer financing)
Explain the options in plain words and how to apply.</p>
<p><strong>Day 14: &quot;Should I close your file?&quot;</strong>
A short, polite email asking whether they still want help. This often gets more replies than any other email, because it asks for a simple yes or no.</p>
<p>After day 14, move the lead into a long term list that gets a helpful email once or twice a month.</p>
<h2>A sample sequence for a med spa consult</h2>
<p><strong>Day 0: &quot;Your consult request at [Med Spa]&quot;</strong> Booking link and what happens at a consult.</p>
<p><strong>Day 1: &quot;Meet your provider&quot;</strong> Credentials, approach and a short personal note.</p>
<p><strong>Day 3: &quot;Does it hurt? Real answers about [treatment]&quot;</strong> Downtime, comfort, results timeline. Keep claims honest and within your medical director&#39;s guidance.</p>
<p><strong>Day 5: &quot;What [client first name] said after her first visit&quot;</strong> A review shared with permission.</p>
<p><strong>Day 8: &quot;What a consult costs (and what it doesn&#39;t)&quot;</strong> Explain any consult fee or deposit and whether it applies toward treatment.</p>
<p><strong>Day 12: &quot;Still thinking about it?&quot;</strong> Offer to answer questions by text.</p>
<h2>Subject lines that work for service businesses</h2>
<ul>
<li>&quot;Quick question about your [project]&quot;</li>
<li>&quot;What to expect at your estimate&quot;</li>
<li>&quot;Photos from a job like yours&quot;</li>
<li>&quot;Is [season] a good time for [service]?&quot;</li>
<li>&quot;Should I close your request?&quot;</li>
</ul>
<p>Avoid all caps, lots of exclamation points and words that sound like a sales flyer.</p>
<h2>Rules you must follow</h2>
<p>The CAN-SPAM Act covers commercial email in the US, including business to business email. The FTC&#39;s compliance guide lists the main requirements:</p>
<ul>
<li>Do not use false or misleading header information or deceptive subject lines.</li>
<li>Identify the message as an ad where it applies.</li>
<li>Include your valid physical postal address.</li>
<li>Tell people how to opt out, and honor opt out requests within 10 business days.</li>
<li>You are responsible for email sent on your behalf, including by an agency.</li>
</ul>
<p>The FTC says each separate email in violation can bring penalties of up to $53,088.</p>
<p>Inbox providers have their own rules too. Google requires every sender to Gmail accounts to set up SPF or DKIM, and bulk senders (more than 5,000 messages a day to Gmail) need SPF, DKIM, DMARC and one click unsubscribe, with spam complaint rates kept below 0.3%. Even if you are a small sender, set up all three records on your domain. It helps your emails land in the inbox.</p>
<h2>Connect email to the rest of your follow up</h2>
<p>Nurture works best inside a CRM where email, text and calls share the same record. That way:</p>
<ul>
<li>When a lead books, every sequence stops.</li>
<li>When a lead replies to an email, your team gets a task to respond.</li>
<li>When a lead clicks the booking link but does not finish, a text can follow up.</li>
<li>You can see which emails lead to booked appointments.</li>
</ul>
<p>Opens are less reliable than they used to be because of privacy features in email apps. Judge your sequence on replies, clicks to the booking page and booked appointments.</p>
<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sending newsletters instead of nurture.</strong> A lead who asked for a quote does not want your company news.</li>
<li><strong>No booking link.</strong> Every email should make the next step one tap away.</li>
<li><strong>Never stopping.</strong> Exclude booked and closed leads automatically.</li>
<li><strong>Writing like a brochure.</strong> Write like you are emailing one person, because you are.</li>
<li><strong>Buying lists.</strong> It hurts deliverability and can break the law.</li>
</ul>
