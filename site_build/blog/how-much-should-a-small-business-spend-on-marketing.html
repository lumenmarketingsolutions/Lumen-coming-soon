How much should a small service business spend on marketing? Set your budget from cost per job

November 14, 2026 · 6 min read · Lumen Marketing

A small service business should set its marketing budget by working backward from the number of new jobs it wants and what it can afford to pay for each one, not from a percentage of revenue picked out of the air. Start with your average gross profit per job, decide the most you are willing to pay in marketing to win a job, estimate your lead to job conversion rate, and multiply. That gives you a budget tied to real economics, and a clear target to judge your marketing against every month.

Why percentage rules fall short

You have probably heard that businesses should spend some fixed percentage of revenue on marketing. Those numbers can be a rough reference point, but they have real problems for a service business:

  • They ignore your margins. A roofer with high ticket jobs and an HVAC company living on service calls have very different economics.
  • They ignore your capacity. If your crew is booked six weeks out, more spend creates leads you cannot serve.
  • They ignore lifetime value. A med spa patient who returns every few months is worth far more than one visit.
  • They do not tell you whether the spend is working.

A budget built from cost per job does all of those things.

Step 1: Know your numbers

Gather these from the last six to twelve months:

  • Average revenue per job (or per new customer)
  • Gross margin after materials, labor and direct costs
  • Lead to appointment rate: what share of leads book an estimate, consult or visit
  • Close rate: what share of appointments become paying jobs
  • Repeat value: how much a typical customer spends over the next year or two

If you do not track these yet, estimate honestly and refine as data comes in.

Step 2: Decide your maximum cost per job

This is the most you are willing to spend on marketing to win one new job. One practical approach is to take a portion of the gross profit from the first job. How much depends on how much repeat business you get and how much profit you need to keep.

Illustrative numbers only.

  • A garage floor job averages $4,500 with $2,000 gross profit. You decide you can spend up to $500 to win one.
  • A med spa new patient spends $600 at the first visit and around $1,500 in the first year, with healthy margin. You might accept $250 to $300 per new patient because of the return visits.
  • An HVAC service call averages $350 with modest margin, but some lead to a system replacement. You might accept $100 per service call booked.

Step 3: Work backward through your funnel

Now turn cost per job into cost per lead.

Allowed cost per lead = maximum cost per job × lead to appointment rate × close rate

Garage floor example:

  • Maximum cost per job: $500
  • Lead to appointment rate: 50%
  • Close rate: 40%
  • Allowed cost per lead: $500 × 0.5 × 0.4 = $100

If your ads bring leads at $60, you have room. If they cost $150, you need to improve the ads, the landing page, the follow up or the sales process before spending more.

Step 4: Set the budget from your job goal

Monthly budget = jobs wanted per month × maximum cost per job

If you want 10 more garage floors per month at up to $500 each, plan for up to $5,000 per month in marketing costs.

Decide whether that number includes agency fees, software and content, or only ad spend. Be consistent, because it changes the math. Many businesses track two numbers: cost per job from ad spend alone, and fully loaded cost per job including fees and tools.

Step 5: Check against capacity

Before committing, make sure you can handle the work:

  • Can your crew handle 10 more jobs a month?
  • Can your office respond to the extra leads within minutes?
  • Do you have the cash flow to pay for ads before jobs are paid?

If capacity is the limit, a smaller budget and better follow up may make you more money than a larger budget.

Step 6: Plan a testing period

New campaigns rarely hit target costs in the first weeks. Ad platforms need data, and you need time to fix the landing page and follow up. Plan a testing period of at least a couple of months with a budget you can afford to learn from, and judge it on trends, not on the first week.

Spreading the budget across channels

Once you know the total, split it by where your customers come from and what each channel costs per job:

  • Google Local Services Ads and Search for people actively looking for your service.
  • Meta ads for projects people plan and services they do not search for yet.
  • Google Business Profile and SEO, which cost time and content more than ad spend, and build over months.
  • Database reactivation and follow up automation, which cost little and often produce the cheapest jobs of all.

Move money toward whichever channel produces jobs below your maximum cost, and away from those that do not. That requires tracking leads to closed sales, which is why tracking should come before scaling.

Seasonality

Service businesses are seasonal. In Idaho and much of the US, HVAC peaks with summer heat and winter cold, landscaping and exterior work follow the weather, and med spas often see busy stretches before holidays and events. Spend more when demand is high and your crew has room, and use quieter months for reactivation, reviews and SEO.

Signs your budget is wrong

  • Too low: campaigns never get enough leads to learn, results swing wildly week to week, and you cannot tell what is working.
  • Too high: leads come in faster than your team can respond, response time slips, and cost per job rises.
  • Right size: you are hitting your cost per job target most months, your team keeps up, and you have room to add more.
