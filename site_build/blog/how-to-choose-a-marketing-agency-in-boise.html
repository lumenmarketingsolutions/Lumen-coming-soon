How to choose a marketing agency in Boise: questions to ask and red flags

November 14, 2026 · 7 min read · Lumen Marketing

To choose a marketing agency in Boise, judge them on three things: how they will measure results in booked jobs and revenue, whether you will own every account they set up, and how clearly they can explain what happens in the first 90 days. A good agency asks more questions about your business than you ask about theirs, talks about cost per booked job instead of likes, and is happy to put its plan and its reporting in writing.

The Treasure Valley has plenty of agencies, from solo freelancers in Meridian to larger shops downtown, plus national firms that sell into Idaho without ever visiting. Many of them do good creative work. Fewer can show you how that work turned into jobs on your calendar. The questions below help you tell the difference in one or two calls.

Bring this list to your meetings and write down the answers.

1. "How will we measure success, and what will you report every month?"

This is the question that matters most. A strong answer names business numbers: leads, booked appointments, jobs sold, cost per booked job and revenue from marketing. It also explains how they will connect a phone call or a form fill to a sale that closes two weeks later in your office or at the customer's kitchen table.

A weak answer leans on reach, impressions, clicks and followers. Those numbers have a place, but they do not tell you whether the phone rang with the right kind of customer.

Good sign: they ask what an average job is worth, what your close rate is, and how many new jobs your crew can handle in a busy week.

Red flag: they promise a number of leads or a ranking position before they have looked at your accounts.

2. "Who owns the ad accounts, website, domain and CRM?"

You should own all of it. Your Google Ads account, Meta ad account and pixel, Google Business Profile, Google Analytics, domain, website hosting and CRM should be in your company's name, with the agency added as a user or partner.

If an agency runs your ads from their own account or registers your domain under their name, leaving them gets painful. You can lose years of ad history, reviews tied to a profile you cannot access, and the website itself.

Good sign: they ask you to create accounts in your name, or they set them up and hand you admin access on day one.

Red flag: "We run everything from our agency account, it's easier."

3. "Who will actually do the work on my account?"

Ask who builds the campaigns, who writes the ads, who answers your questions and how many other accounts that person manages. Some agencies sell with a senior person and hand the work to someone junior or overseas. That can work if it is supervised, but you deserve to know.

4. "What will you do in the first 30, 60 and 90 days?"

You want a plan, even a simple one. For a typical Boise service business it might look like this:

  • First 30 days: audit current accounts, fix call and form tracking, clean up the Google Business Profile, launch the first campaigns and set up automatic text follow up for new leads.
  • Days 30 to 60: cut keywords and ads that bring the wrong people, improve the landing page, test new offers and creative.
  • Days 60 to 90: move budget toward what produces booked jobs, add a second channel if the first is working, start a reactivation campaign to past customers.

An agency that cannot describe its first month will be figuring it out on your budget.

5. "What happens after a lead comes in?"

Most marketing problems in home services and med spas are follow up problems. Leads that wait hours for a call back often book with someone else. Ask how the agency handles that gap. Do they set up instant text replies, missed call text back, reminders and a pipeline that shows which leads were contacted? Or does their job end when the form is submitted?

Good sign: they want to listen to a few of your recorded calls or see how your office handles inquiries.

6. "Can you show me results from a business like mine?"

Ask for a case study or a screen share of a real account with private details hidden. Look for numbers over several months, ideally cost per lead and cost per booked job, not a single screenshot of a good week. Also ask about something that did not work and what they changed. Honest agencies have those stories and will tell them.

7. "What is the contract, and how do I leave?"

A three month minimum is common and reasonable, since campaigns need time to gather data. A twelve month lock in with no exit clause is a lot to ask from someone you have never worked with. Ask what happens to your accounts, creative and data if you cancel.

8. "How is your fee structured, and is ad spend separate?"

Your ad spend should be billed by Google and Meta directly to your card, so you can see exactly what was spent. The agency fee should be a separate, clear number. Be careful with packages that bundle "up to $500 of ads" into a flat fee, because you cannot see how much actually reached the platforms.

9. "Do you know the local market?"

A Boise agency does not need to be down the street, but they should understand the Treasure Valley. Search behavior in Eagle and Star is different from Nampa and Caldwell. Seasonality matters a lot here: HVAC demand swings with hot summers and cold snaps, landscaping and epoxy floor work follow the weather, and roofing spikes after hail. Ask how they plan for that.

A worked example: comparing two proposals

The proposals below are hypothetical, to show how to compare them.

You run a roofing company in Meridian and get two proposals.

Agency A charges $1,500 per month. They promise "500+ new leads a year," 12 social posts per month and a monthly report on impressions and clicks. Ads run from their account.

Agency B charges $2,500 per month plus ad spend billed to your card. They will set up call tracking, connect your CRM so leads move through stages from new to sold, launch Google search ads and Local Services Ads, add automatic text follow up, and report every month on cost per inspection booked and cost per roof sold. They ask what an average roof is worth and what share of inspections you close.

Agency A looks cheaper and promises more. But you would have no way to tell whether any of those 500 leads became a roof. With Agency B, if your average roof brings in $4,000 of gross profit, you can see in the report exactly how many roofs the program needs to sell each month to pay for itself, and whether it is getting there.

The useful question is which proposal ties itself to the number that pays your bills.

Red flags worth walking away from

  • They guarantee rankings, a lead count or a return before seeing your accounts.
  • They will not put accounts, domain or website in your name.
  • Reporting is only reach, impressions and clicks.
  • They cannot explain how they track phone calls back to the ad that caused them.
  • Every client gets the same package regardless of industry.
  • They are slow to answer during the sales process. It rarely gets better after you sign.

What good looks like

The right agency feels like part of your team. They tell you when something is not working before you notice it. They explain things in plain words, care about what happens after the click, and want to hear about lead quality from the people answering your phones.
